\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:clustering_agent_representation_conclusion}

Taken together, the high \AbstractionScoreName and the stable \ClusteringUniversalityName obtained across all five environments provide empirical support for the \RepresentationConvergenceHypothesisName.
These findings suggest that the three hypotheses investigated in this manuscript, \MechanisticHypothesisName, \ConceptHypothesisName, and \RepresentationConvergenceHypothesisName, provide a promising research direction for understanding the structure of learned representations in \gls{rl} agents.

Beyond these results, \gls{car} provides a general and automated framework for analysing agent representations.
Both the analysis procedure and the metrics are fully automated, requiring no manual intervention or environment-specific adjustments.
This makes \gls{car} a general-purpose method that can be applied to a wide range of \gls{rl} settings while providing a data-driven characterization of the structure of learned representations.

However, these results should be interpreted with some caution.
First, the experiments were conducted on a limited set of architectures and environments, and the broader robustness of the findings remains to be established.
In addition, several methodological choices were made for simplicity, including the choice of the \gls{ls} layer, the Euclidean distance used to compare embeddings, and the K-means clustering algorithm.
The influence of these choices on the discovered structure has not yet been systematically evaluated.
Consequently, further experiments are needed to determine whether the observed convergence reflects the underlying \gls{cs} of the agents or is partly influenced by these methodological choices.

A second major limitation concerns the semantic interpretation of the discovered clusters.
While \gls{car} provides a quantitative description of the structure present in the agent's representations, the semantics assigned to individual clusters currently rely on manual interpretation.
For example, the labels associated with the clusters in Figure~\ref{fig:semantic_granularity} represent our interpretation of the discovered structure.
This process is time-consuming and potentially subjective, and it does not provide a systematic guarantee that the assigned semantics accurately describe the underlying agent behavior.
Developing methods to automatically extract the semantics of each cluster, together with reliable metrics to evaluate the correctness of these interpretations, is therefore an important direction for future work.

These limitations directly motivate several avenues for future research.
First, extending \gls{car} to other architectures and environments, including continuous-control and real-world domains, would provide a stronger test of the generality of the observed convergence.
Second, systematically studying the impact of the selected \gls{ls} layer, distance measures, and clustering algorithms would clarify how much the discovered structure depends on these methodological choices.
Alternative clustering paradigms, such as density-based or hierarchical approaches, could also provide complementary views of the underlying representation structure.
Finally, automating cluster interpretation and developing human-centered evaluation protocols would strengthen both the semantic validity and the practical relevance of the resulting explanations.

Overall, \gls{car} provides a scalable, data-driven, and quantitatively grounded framework for studying the structure of agent representations.
By revealing stable and interpretable structures across multiple environments, it constitutes a step toward the intrinsic, mechanistic explainability advocated in this manuscript.
