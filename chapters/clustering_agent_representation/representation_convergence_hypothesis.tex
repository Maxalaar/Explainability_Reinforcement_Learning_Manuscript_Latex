\section{Representation Convergence Hypothesis}
\label{sec:representation_convergence_hypothesis}

The Clustering Agent Representations (CAR) method relies on a assumption, referred to as the Representation Convergence Hypothesis. 
This hypothesis follows the two assumptions established previously throughout this manuscript.

The first assumption rejects the notion that deep neural networks constitute irreducible black boxes \ref{subsec:explanation}.
Adopting a mechanistic perspective, we consider that every process results from a sequence of causal computations.
Although these computations may be highly complex, they remain explainable through the analysis of the internal mechanisms.

The second assumption states that neural networks achieve generalization by learning concepts \ref{sec:concept_hypothesis}.
During optimization, the network progressively organizes its latent space into representations that capture the information required to solve the task.

Following these hypotheses, it becomes possible to explain the \gls{nn}'s process by understanding the organization of its latent space.
This family of approaches is commonly referred to as mechanistic interpretability.

In mechanistic interpretability, the consistency with which a concept is recovered across different models or independent extractions is referred to as its universality \cite{templeton2024scaling}.
A concept is said to be universal if it repeatedly emerges despite variations in model initialization, optimization, or training conditions.

The Representation Convergence Hypothesis provides a theoretical explanation for this phenomenon.
We hypothesize that, for a given task and a given neural network architecture, there exists a privileged conceptual organization.
This organization naturally emerges because it constitutes an efficient representation of the information required to solve the task.
Under this assumption, the universality of a concept is not merely an empirical observation but rather the expected consequence of a common underlying conceptual structure shared by independently learned representations.

It is important to clarify that this hypothesis does not imply convergence at the parameter level.
Independently trained models are expected to converge toward different sets of weights.
The proposed hypothesis concerns a more abstract level of representation.
We assume that, although the numerical implementation of the networks may differ, the conceptual organization induced by their \gls{lr} converges toward an equivalent conceptual structure.
Consequently, the objective is not to compare neural parameters or latent activations directly, but compare the underlying conceptual structure encoded within the latent space.

The remainder of this chapter builds upon this hypothesis.
This principle forms the theoretical foundation of the \ClusteringUniversalityName metric introduced later in Subsecstion~\ref{subsec:clustering_universality}.
