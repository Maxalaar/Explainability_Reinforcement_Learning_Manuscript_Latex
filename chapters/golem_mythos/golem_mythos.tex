A creation that surpasses its creator.

This idea has long captured the imagination and continues to resonate in contemporary society.
Long before modern technology, humanity had already questioned whether its creations might escape control.

One of the earliest expressions of this idea can be found in the story of the Golem.
In Hebrew tradition, the term Golem refers to an artificial creature made of clay and created by humans to serve them \cite{nocks1998golem}.
Figure~\ref{fig:golem_creation} illustrates the creation of such a servant.
The earliest accounts describe the Golem as a protective and helpful figure, reflecting the idea that humans can create an artificial being for the benefit of society.

This vision becomes more ambiguous in the seventeenth century, as illustrated by the legend of the Golem of Prague.
In this story, the initially small Golem gradually grows in size and strength until it can no longer be controlled \cite{nocks1998golem}.
The creation intended to protect others therefore becomes a potential threat to them.
The only remaining solution is to destroy the creature in order to protect the community.

This narrative motif has been revisited in different forms to illustrate the risks inherent in creation.
This continuity can be seen in the emergence of the modern notion of the robot.
The concept of the robot as an artificial servant was introduced in the play \textit{Rossum's Universal Robots}, written in 1920 \cite{capek1920rur}.
In the play, a scientist creates artificial beings designed to perform labour.
Conceived as servants, these artificial beings turn against humanity and destroy civilisation.
The connection between the Golem and the robot was explicitly made by the author, as illustrated by his statement that “the Robot is the Golem made flesh through mass production” \cite{comrada1995golem}.
Despite their destructive role in the play, the word robot is now used to designate artificial beings created to serve humans.
From its very origin, the word robot has embodied the hopes and fears surrounding technological progress.

Today, this tension between the hopes and fears surrounding technological progress is particularly evident in the development of artificial intelligence.
It is therefore not surprising that this field offers an almost literal modern adaptation of the Golem myth.
Artificial systems are created to automate tasks traditionally associated with human intelligence.
Their rapid development in recent years has transformed a wide range of domains, further intensifying both excitement and concerns surrounding this progress.

Among the subfields of artificial intelligence, reinforcement learning is specifically concerned with the development of autonomous agents.
In this learning paradigm, an artificial agent interacts with an environment, selects actions, and receives feedback according to the outcomes of its behaviour \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}.
Through repeated interaction with its environment, the agent learns a strategy to achieve the objective.
A strength of this approach is its ability to produce autonomous systems with minimal human intervention.
Their agent behavior is not programmed but learned through interactions with the environment \cite{amodei2016concrete}.
This minimal human guidance enables reinforcement learning systems to achieve superhuman capabilities \cite{silver2017mastering, openai2019dota2largescale}.

However, maintaining control over such agents presents a major challenge, as the very autonomy that makes this learning approach powerful also makes their behavior more difficult to control.
Despite the difficulty of this task, understanding the behaviour of such agents is therefore the main objective of this manuscript.
This work therefore places itself within a long-standing line of thought that can be summarized by the following question: how can we ensure that our creations remain aligned with human intentions?
