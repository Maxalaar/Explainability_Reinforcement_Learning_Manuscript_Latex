\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:deep_learning_conclusion}

This chapter introduced the essential building blocks of \gls{dl}: the historical developments that led to its emergence, a formalization of its objective as function approximation, the structure of a \gls{nn}, the gradient-based procedure used to train it, and the criteria used to evaluate the resulting model.
Together, these elements provide the foundation required for the rest of this manuscript.

\gls{dl} is, however, a considerably broader field than what has been covered here, and one that keeps expanding at a fast pace.
To keep the presentation digestible, several simplifications were made throughout this chapter: the \gls{mlp} was used as the sole architecture, the learning objective was restricted to a regression setting with the \gls{mse} loss, and training relied on vanilla \gls{sgd} rather than any of its more elaborate variants.
These choices were made deliberately, to expose the core mechanisms of \gls{dl} as clearly as possible, not to provide an exhaustive account of the field.
This chapter should therefore be understood as an introduction laying the groundwork for the rest of the manuscript, rather than a comprehensive treatment of \gls{dl}.

Despite this simplicity, the underlying principle is not: a \gls{nn} is nothing more than a composition of simple units, each computing a weighted sum followed by a nonlinearity, whose parameters are adjusted by following the gradient of a loss function.
Yet the composition of many such simple units, and their joint optimization, gives rise to functions expressive enough to approximate highly complex mappings.
This gap between the simplicity of the underlying principle and the complexity of what it produces is a central reason why \gls{nn} remain difficult to fully understand.

This difficulty is compounded by the lack of theoretical guarantees surrounding \gls{dl}.
As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:learning}, gradient descent is not guaranteed to converge to a global optimum once the loss is non-convex, and, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}, there is no complete theoretical account of why trained networks generalize beyond their training data.
Nevertheless, the empirical successes recalled in Section~\ref{sec:historical_developments} show that \gls{dl} works remarkably well in practice, across an increasingly wide range of domains.
This tension between limited theoretical understanding and strong empirical performance is one of the main motivations for the rest of this manuscript.

A first step toward reducing this gap is to look inside the network itself.
As noted in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}, a network's ability to generalize stems from the internal representations that its hidden layers progressively construct during training.
The next chapter is devoted to studying the structure of these internal representations, referred to as the \gls{ls}.
