\section{Experimental Configuration}
\label{sec:experiments_configuration}

This section describes the configuration of the experiments reported in this chapter.
It covers the environments, the neural architecture of the policies, and the main parameters of each pipeline stage.
In general, for each environment, a single initial policy is trained and then imitated by three surrogate policies.

\subsection{Environments}

All experiments use environments from the Atari benchmark \cite{Bellemare_2013}, accessed through the Arcade Learning Environment.
The use of Atari environments offers several advantages.
First, Atari is a widely used benchmark in the \gls{rl} community, with many studies using these environments as standard evaluation settings.
This makes the experimental results easier to compare with existing work.
Second, these environments were not specifically designed for \gls{rl}, which reduces the risk of introducing biases related to the design of the environments themselves.
Finally, the benchmark provides a diverse set of well-known environments with varying levels of complexity.
This diversity makes it possible to evaluate \gls{car} across different types of tasks and levels of difficulty.

The configuration of the Atari environments follows common practice in \gls{rl} research \cite{machado2017revisitingarcadelearningenvironment}.

In each environment, the agent receives visual observations in the form of raw pixels.
Each image is resized to $64 \times 64$ pixels and converted to grayscale to reduce memory requirements.
An exception is made for Pac-Man, where color information is important for determining the behavior of other agents in the environment.
The original color information is therefore preserved for this environment.

To provide information about the dynamics of the environment, four consecutive observations are stacked along the channel dimension.
The resulting observation has a shape of $64 \times 64 \times 4$.
This allows the agent to infer the motion of objects from successive observations rather than relying on a single frame.

Frame skipping is also used to reduce the duration of each episode.
The agent selects a new action every five frames, while the selected action is repeated for the intervening frames.
This provides sufficient temporal resolution for effective control while reducing the number of decisions required during an episode.

The actions correspond to the different buttons available on the Atari controller.
The resulting action space is therefore discrete.
Since Atari games are fully deterministic, sticky actions are introduced to add stochasticity to the trajectories.
With a probability of $0.05$, the environment repeats the action selected at the previous step instead of applying the new action.
This prevents the agent from simply memorizing a fixed sequence of actions during training.

\paragraph{Breakout.}
The agent moves a paddle at the bottom of the screen (Figure~\ref{fig:environment_breakout}) to bounce a ball into a wall of bricks, each broken brick scoring a point, and the ball speeding up as the wall is cleared.

\paragraph{Mario Bros.}
A single-screen platform game (Figure~\ref{fig:environment_mario_bros}).
The player clears each phase by hitting enemies from below to flip them, then kicking them off the platforms before they recover.
A central POW block flips every enemy at once but can only be used a few times per phase.

\paragraph{Ms.\ Pac-Man.}
The player moves through a maze (Figure~\ref{fig:environment_ms_pacman}), eating pellets while avoiding four ghosts, with power pellets briefly making the ghosts edible.
The maze structure and the many possible local situations, such as a nearby ghost, an intersection, or an active power pellet, produce a large number of distinguishable states.

\paragraph{Pong.}
A minimal two-player game (Figure~\ref{fig:environment_pong}) in which the agent controls one paddle and must send the ball past the opponent.

\paragraph{Space Invaders.}
The agent moves a cannon along the bottom of the screen (Figure~\ref{fig:environment_space_invaders}), firing at descending rows of aliens while dodging their shots and using destructible shields, with a bonus saucer occasionally crossing the top.

\paragraph{Surround.}
A two-player game on a grid (Figure~\ref{fig:environment_surround}).
Each player leaves a solid trail behind a moving cursor, and the goal is to force the opponent to crash into a trail or a wall.

\subsection{Neural architecture}

The initial policy and the surrogate policies are convolutional networks that share the same feature-extraction trunk.
The trunk is a sequence of four convolutional layers, with $16$, $32$, $64$, and $128$ channels, $4 \times 4$ kernels, and a stride of $2$, each followed by layer normalization and a LeakyReLU activation.
Its input is the observation, a stack of four consecutive $64 \times 64$ frames.

The initial policy follows an actor--critic design: the trunk feeds two separate multilayer perceptrons, one producing the parameters of the action distribution and one producing the value estimate.
The surrogate policy has the encoder--decoder structure introduced in Section~\ref{sec:clustering_agent_representation_method}: its encoder $\textcolor{myblue}{f_{enc}}$ is a trunk with the same architecture, and its decoder is a single multilayer perceptron producing the parameters of the action distribution.
The surrogate has no value head, since it is trained only to reproduce the action distribution of the initial policy.

The latent space analyzed by \gls{car} is the representation produced by the last convolutional layer of the surrogate encoder.
