\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:experiments_conclusion}

The quantitative results show that \gls{car} often recovers similar clusterings across independently trained surrogate policies.
In many of the tested configurations, the \gls{cu} exceeds $0.5$, indicating that the latent representations developed by different policies contain sufficiently similar structures to produce comparable partitions \cite{amezquita2020orchestrating}.
This result supports the \RepresentationConvergenceHypothesisName, which assumes that independently trained networks tend to develop similar conceptual organizations when solving the same task.
However, \gls{cu} generally decreases with the depth of the network.
The stability of the representations is therefore progressively reduced in deeper layers.

The \gls{as} provides an important complement to this observation.
The earliest layers tend to achieve high \gls{cu}, but also exhibit low levels of abstraction because their representations remain strongly related to the observations, the action distribution, and the initial network structure.
Considering \gls{cu} alone would therefore favor the earliest layers without necessarily identifying the most meaningful representations.
The \gls{acu} addresses this limitation by combining consistency and abstraction into a single measure.
It therefore provides a practical criterion for identifying regions of the latent space that offer a useful balance between these two properties.
The metric is also fully automated and can be applied consistently across different environments and network configurations.
Increasing the number of clusters generally reduces \gls{acu}, as finer partitions tend to be less stable across surrogate policies.
At the same time, these finer partitions reveal more specific distinctions between situations encountered during the simulations.
This highlights a trade-off between the stability of the recovered concepts and the level of detail captured by the clustering.

The qualitative analysis complements these quantitative results by showing that the identified clusters can correspond to interpretable properties of the environment and of the agent's behavior.
Across the different environments, clusters capture recurring aspects such as the progression of an episode, specific game situations, and patterns related to the agent's behavior.
Increasing the number of clusters generally produces a finer description of these situations, although the resulting distinctions are often less consistently recovered across surrogate policies.
Together, these results support the assumptions underlying \gls{car} and show that the proposed metrics provide a practical way to identify and compare meaningful latent-space organizations across independently trained policies.
