\section{Summary of Contributions}
\label{sec:conclusion_summary_of_contributions}

This section revisits the four research questions introduced in Section~\ref{sec:introduction_research_questions}.
It is organized around the three contributions of this thesis, each of which is presented in a dedicated subsection.
A final subsection then brings together the hypotheses formulated throughout this manuscript and shows how they form a coherent chain.

\subsection{Survey of Latent Space Analysis Methods}

Research Question~\ref{rq:latent_space} concerned the approaches proposed in the literature to analyze the internal workings of \glspl{nn}.
To answer it, we first made explicit the working hypothesis shared by most of this literature, the \ConceptHypothesisName.
According to this hypothesis, \glspl{nn} generalize by organizing their \glspl{ls} around concepts.
We then organized existing methods into four families, namely probing, interpretable directions, \glspl{sae}, and clustering.

This review shows that the first three families assume that concepts can be recovered from \glspl{lr} through a linear decomposition.
Clustering is the only family that does not rely on this assumption, which makes it the basis of the method proposed in this thesis.
The review also reveals that there is no consensus on how to evaluate whether these methods truly capture the concepts of a model.

\subsection{Taxonomy of Explainable Reinforcement Learning Methods}

Research Question~\ref{rq:taxonomy} concerned the organization of existing \gls{xrl} methods \parencite{alaarabiou:hal-04685607, alaarabiou2026taxonomy}.
Our starting point was to clarify what an explanation means in the context of \gls{rl}.
Building on this framework, we characterized each explanation by its subject, its source, and its form.
The subject distinguishes the explanation of a single decision from that of the agent's strategy, which is specific to the temporal nature of \gls{rl}.
From these elements, we proposed a taxonomy that accounts for the specificities of \gls{rl} and used it to review the state of the art.

This review led to three main observations.
None of the reviewed methods explains the learning process itself.
The field lacks unified metrics for evaluating explanations.
Finally, current research remains dominated by substitutive approaches, which replace the deep policy with a simpler model and thus lose part of what makes it effective.
These observations point to the need for an intrinsic and objectively evaluated method.

\subsection{The CAR Method and Its Evaluation}

Research Questions~\ref{rq:mechanisms} and~\ref{rq:evaluation} concerned the use of \glspl{lr} to explain \gls{rl} agents and the objective evaluation of such explanations.
To address them, we introduced \gls{car}, an \gls{xrl} method based on the principles of \gls{me} \parencite{alaarabiou2026car}.
\gls{car} independently trains several surrogate policies to reproduce the same behavior, then clusters their \glspl{lr} and compares the resulting partitions.
This procedure relies on the \RepresentationConvergenceHypothesisName, according to which networks trained on the same task develop similar concepts.
To evaluate it, we introduced three metrics.
The \gls{cu} measures the consistency of the clusters across surrogate policies, the \gls{as} measures how much they differ from trivial baselines, and the \gls{acu} combines both properties.

Applied to six Atari environments, \gls{car} recovers similar clustering structures across independently trained surrogate policies, with a \gls{cu} that frequently exceeds $0.5$.
This convergence reflects concepts that emerge during learning, since the \gls{as} verifies that the clusters are abstract and do not merely reproduce the structure of the observations, the actions, or an untrained network.
This interpretation is confirmed by the qualitative analysis, which shows that the clusters capture interpretable aspects of the task, such as the progression of the episode or specific game situations.
Overall, these results provide empirical support for the \RepresentationConvergenceHypothesisName.

\subsection{A Coherent Chain of Hypotheses}

Throughout these contributions, we developed three hypotheses that form a coherent chain, each one building on the previous one.
The \MechanisticHypothesisName states that \glspl{nn} can be understood through their internal mechanisms.
It justifies analyzing the network itself rather than replacing it with a simpler model.
The \ConceptHypothesisName states that these mechanisms are organized around concepts.
It specifies what this analysis should look for in the \gls{ls}.
The \RepresentationConvergenceHypothesisName states that these concepts reflect the task.
It provides a criterion to determine whether the identified concepts are reliable, since concepts shared by independently trained networks cannot be attributed to a particular model.

These three hypotheses do not have the same status.
The first two are working hypotheses widely adopted in the literature, which this thesis does not attempt to demonstrate.
The third one is directly put to the test by \gls{car}, and is therefore both the foundation of the method and the object of its investigation.
The experimental results support it, without constituting a formal proof.

This chain also changes the role of universality.
In the literature, the convergence of representations across models is mainly reported as an empirical observation.
In this thesis, it becomes a principle of explanation, as concepts that emerge in every model trained on a task characterize the task itself.
It is this principle that makes it possible to evaluate explanations objectively, without relying on human judgment.
